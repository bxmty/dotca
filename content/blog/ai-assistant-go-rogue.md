Short answer: yes. An AI assistant that can act on its own can make a mistake, get tricked, or be taken over by someone else. You can lower that risk a lot with a few simple rules, and none of them need a technical background.

---

## Why we're talking about this now

A new kind of AI tool is showing up in everyday business software. Older AI tools wrote text when you asked. These newer ones, often called "agents," can act. They can read your email, move files, update records and send messages, with little or no one watching.

This week, Microsoft said companies should treat these agents like employees on the inside, not like trusted software. It pointed out that agents are often given far more access than they need. Microsoft was careful to add that this doesn't mean agents are out to cause harm. They "can make mistakes, be manipulated, or be compromised."

The other side of the story is less comfortable. Researchers found a criminal using AI agents to break into company websites with almost no human effort. The agents tried more than 1,200 sites and got into websites belonging to more than 100 companies. It cost the attacker roughly $50 per successful break-in.

The same technology is available to you and to the people trying to get past you.

---

## What an AI assistant really is

Think of it as a new hire with its own login. It works at all hours and is very fast. It doesn't stop to ask whether something seems odd, and it does what it's told, even if the instruction hides inside an email or a web page.

You wouldn't give a new hire the keys to everything on their first day. The same rule applies here.

---

## Four questions to ask before you switch one on

**1. What can it see?**
List what the assistant could read: email, client files, accounting, shared drives. If the answer is "everything," that's the first thing to fix. Give it only what it needs for the job in front of it.

**2. What can it change?**
Reading is one thing. Sending, deleting and paying are another. Anything that moves money, sends something to a client, or removes records should need a person to say yes first.

**3. Can we undo it?**
Before it starts, know how you would put things back. That means a recent backup you've actually tested. If a mistake can't be reversed, the assistant shouldn't be doing that job.

**4. Who do we call if it goes wrong?**
Decide now who can switch it off and who needs to be told. Government security agencies in the US publish guidance with the same five steps: prepare, notice, respond, recover and report. Their document is aimed at the companies that build advanced AI, not at small businesses, but the order still applies to you.

---

## The honest downside

Rules like these slow things down a little. Someone has to approve the risky actions, and someone has to check what the assistant can reach. Some of the convenience that made the tool attractive goes away.

For routine work, that trade is easy to skip. For anything touching client, financial or legal information, it's worth it.
