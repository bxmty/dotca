Short answer: probably, yes. Surveys keep finding that many people use AI at work with no training and no clear rule, and some would paste a client's contract into a personal account to save time. A written rule helps. On its own, it isn't enough.

---

## What we're seeing

A security company called Huntress recently surveyed working adults about AI at work. Here is what it found:

- 43% said they had no formal training on using AI at work.
- 26% said their company had no policy or guidance of any kind.
- Only 24% said their company had a clear policy that had been communicated to them.
- About a quarter said they would paste a client contract into a personal AI account to get a summary.

Two things to know before you take those numbers to heart. The survey covered US workers at companies with 50 or more employees (501 people, with a margin of error of about 4 points), run by a research firm for Huntress in July 2026. Your firm is probably smaller and in Canada. And Huntress sells security software, so it has a reason to care about the answer.

We haven't measured this for firms your size, so we won't tell you your numbers look like these. If anything, we'd expect a small firm to be no better prepared than a bigger one, but we haven't measured that either.

---

## Why people do it

Almost nobody is trying to cause trouble. They have a long document to get through, a client waiting, and a free tool that does it in thirty seconds.

Often the approved route is the slow one. The work account has signed them out, the second sign-in step is a hassle, and a personal tab is already open. If your team is using something you haven't approved, treat it as information. It tells you where the official way of working isn't working. Fix that first, before you blame anyone.

---

## What's actually at risk

Keep this concrete.

A client's contract, a set of financials or a list of customer details pasted into a personal AI account sits outside any agreement your business has with the provider. Paid business plans from the big providers typically say in their terms that they don't train on your data by default. OpenAI says so for ChatGPT Business, for example. Free personal accounts usually have different terms, and many people never read them. Check the current terms for whichever tool you use.

To be fair about it: using a personal account does not prove the data was used to train anything, or that anyone else saw it. The honest problem is that you can't tell, and neither can your client. That is hard to explain if a client ever asks.

There is also a second risk that has nothing to do with pasting. An AI tool that reads a web page or a document can be fooled by hidden text in it that tells the tool to do something else. Training should cover that too, and not only how to write a good request.

In Canada, the federal privacy commissioner's guidance on generative AI is clear that the business, not the tool, stays responsible for personal information it handles. That means choosing tools whose terms you have actually read, and keeping personal information out of prompts where you can. The commissioner also says to enter sensitive or confidential information only where authorised.

This post is not legal advice. If your work involves health, financial or other sensitive records, or your province has its own privacy law, ask your lawyer what applies to your firm, and check any contracts that say how client information must be handled.

## Why a one-page rule isn't enough

You will hear two opposing views, and both sellers have something to sell.

Huntress says: write the policy and make sure staff have seen it. Sophos, another security company, says a policy document on its own doesn't change what happens, and that you need technical controls (software that blocks or logs what goes into AI tools) to make the rule true.
Our view is somewhere in the middle. Write the rule first. It costs almost nothing, and most firms don't have one. But a rule nobody can follow easily gets ignored, so pair it with a tool people actually want to use. Add monitoring only if the size of your risk justifies it. For most small firms, a clear rule and a good approved tool do most of the work.

A rule is where you start, not where you stop. Feeling confident is not the same as behaving safely.

## Four things to do this week

**1. Find out what's in use.**
Ask your team, without blame, which AI tools they use and what for, including on their phones. Then check what the company is paying for: subscriptions, browser add-ons, anything on a company card. People will tell you more if the question is "what's helping you?" instead of "what did you do?"
_Why it helps you:_ you can't make a sensible rule until you know what people are actually doing. We can do the "what are we paying for" part for you, in an AI tool audit. You'd get a one-page list of what's in use and what it costs.

**2. Give people an approved tool that works.**
Pick one paid business version of an AI tool, not the free personal one, and set it up so signing in is easy. Before you buy, check three things: the provider says in writing that it won't use your business data to train its models, one person (you or someone you trust) can add and remove users, and it works on the phones your people carry. This does more than any rule to remove the reason for a personal account.

As a rough guide, the business plans from OpenAI (ChatGPT Business), Anthropic (Claude Team) and Microsoft (Copilot Business, added to a Microsoft 365 Business plan) run about US$20 to $25 per person per month. Those are list prices in October 2026, in US dollars, before tax, so expect the Canadian figure to differ. They are examples, not endorsements. Check current prices before you buy. If you already pay for Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, ask what AI is included before adding anything.
_Why it helps you:_ it puts your data under an agreement you have actually read, and it makes the safe path the easy path. If you want help choosing, we can do that, and we'll tell you if the tool you already have is good enough.

**3. Write a one-page rule in plain language.**
Say what's fine, what needs a yes first (client files, financial records, contracts, anything with personal details, and photos of any of them), and what's never allowed. Say who to ask. Say what the approved tool may be used for without asking, so the safe tool stays the easy one. You can [download our one-page rule for your staff](/resources/ai-use-rule) as a PDF or an editable Word file, along with a separate set of notes for you as the owner. Fill in the blanks and change it to suit your firm.
_Why it helps you:_ people follow a short rule they can read in two minutes.

**4. Train for the real risks, then check back.**
Spend about twenty minutes on what not to paste or photograph, how to spot a fake AI tool, and what a hidden-text trick looks like. Ask again in three months. We can run the session for you.
_Why it helps you:_ it covers the gap a written rule leaves, and it gets people used to asking before they paste.

## If you find someone already did it

Stay calm. This is a process problem more often than a people problem. In this order:

1. Find out what was shared, and which account it went into.
2. Ask why they didn't use the work account. The answer shows you what to fix.
3. Respond in proportion to what was shared. A public price list is not the same as a client's personal details. If client personal information was involved, get advice about whether you need to tell anyone.
4. Fix the way of working so it doesn't happen again: better tool, clearer rule, short training.
5. Write down what you confirmed, and only that.

## The honest downside

None of this is free. A business AI account costs money for every person who uses it, and that adds up in a team of twenty. A rule slows people down a little, because someone has to be asked before certain things go in. If you add monitoring on top, it costs more again, and some staff will feel watched. That feeling is real, and it can sour a team if you don't explain why you're doing it.

For most small firms, we think a clear rule, one good approved tool and a short training session is the right amount. We'd rather tell you that than sell you more.
